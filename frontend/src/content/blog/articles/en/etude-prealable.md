Where do you start, and in what order? When the authorities talk to you about a ‘preliminary study’ (étude préalable) before works on your historic monument, the process can quickly seem like a maze: one more document to produce, an architect to find, a budget to set aside before the first stone has even been laid. The good news: this stage follows a simple logic and, done well, it saves you time and money on the rest of the project. Here is how to go about it, without missing anything.

## What is a preliminary study for?

The **preliminary study** is the assessment you commission *before* starting restoration works on a property protected as a historic monument. It brings together the historical, scientific and technical expertise that describes the actual condition of your property and defines what needs to be done, and how.

In practice, it answers three questions. What is the existing fabric worth, and what condition is it really in? Which interventions are needed, and which should be ruled out? And according to what programme, in what order, at what cost? It is the document that turns an intention (‘I want to restore my roof’) into a workable project.

This study is not a pedantic formality. On a listed (classé) property, the State departments responsible for historic monuments exercise **scientific and technical oversight** from this early stage: they share the knowledge they hold and specify, depending on the nature and complexity of the works, which studies must be carried out before the programme is settled. In other words, the preliminary study is also the moment when your project and the authorities get aligned, before everything is set in stone.

:::remarque
‘Preliminary study’ and ‘diagnostic’ refer to the same idea: establishing a reasoned assessment of the existing situation before acting. Depending on who you are dealing with, you will hear one term or the other. Either way, the aim is the same: know before you act.
:::

## When should you start it?

The right reflex is to start it **early**, well before the first pickaxe swings. The preliminary study sits at the very start of the project, ahead of the application for authorisation and, naturally, of the works themselves. It then feeds into the detailed design, then the authorisation, then the works.

A reassuring point: the preliminary study is **exempt from authorisation**. You can have it carried out without waiting for the authorities’ formal approval of the works (that approval will only come once the project has been fully worked out). The study phase is therefore the right time to explore, measure, even hesitate, without holding up the timetable.

:::frise
- **Step 1**: preliminary study (assessment of the existing fabric and programme of works)
- **Step 2**: architectural and technical design of the project by the project manager (maître d’œuvre)
- **Step 3**: application for works authorisation (and grant application)
- **Step 4**: carrying out the works, under the State’s scientific and technical oversight
- **Step 5**: handover of the works and final review
:::

:::attention[The timing trap]
A State grant must be requested **before** you start what it pays for, usually in the preceding year. If you want funding for the study *and* funding for the works, be aware that you will in principle need **two separate applications**: one for the preliminary study, one for the works. Starting a phase before submitting the corresponding application can cost you the funding.
:::

## The steps, in order

The sequence below applies mainly to a **listed** property, where oversight is strongest. For a **registered** (inscrit) property, the logic is the same, in a lighter form.

:::etapes{display="colonnes"}
1. **Contact the DRAC**: early on (the DRAC is the Ministry of Culture’s regional office), to scope the project, find out what is already known and which studies are expected.
2. **Choose the project manager**: the architect who will carry out the study, chosen according to the qualifications required for your property.
3. **Have the preliminary study carried out**: assessment of the existing fabric, expert reports, programme and cost estimate for the works.
4. **Submit the grant application for the study**: to the DRAC, before the study work starts.
5. **Refine the project and apply for authorisation**: once the programme is settled, the project manager designs the project and you apply for works authorisation.
:::

Who carries out the study? That depends on the status of your property.

- For a **listed building not owned by the State**, you are free to choose your project manager from among **qualified** architects: an architecte en chef des monuments historiques (ACMH, chief architect of historic monuments), or an architect holding a specialist diploma in architecture and heritage together with significant experience in restoring old buildings.
- For a **listed building owned by the State**, the designated project manager is the **architecte en chef des monuments historiques**.
- For a **registered building**, the regulations impose no specialist qualification; nevertheless, make sure you entrust the study to a professional who has already handled comparable projects.

:::remarque
The architecte en chef des monuments historiques (ACMH) is an architect recruited by competitive examination, a State official specialising in the conservation of protected heritage. On a publicly owned listed property, their involvement is the rule; on a privately owned listed property, they are one of the qualified profiles you can choose.
:::

## What it costs

The preliminary study has its own cost, separate from that of the works. The project manager is paid fees for this assignment, most often at a fixed price agreed in advance. The amount depends on the size of the monument, its complexity and the extent of the surveys to be produced: there is no single scale of fees.

The breakdown below is **an illustrative example** of how the budget for a study assignment is split. Check each line with your project manager and your DRAC, as the orders of magnitude vary widely from one project to another.

:::cout{unit="€"}
- Surveys and condition report: 4000
- Assessment and expert reports (historical, scientific, technical): 6000
- Programme and cost estimate for the works: 3000
:::

The good news on funding: **preliminary diagnostic studies** for restoration are eligible for State grants for protected buildings, in the same way as works. As always, funding is requested from the DRAC and **before** the study starts. The spending threshold below which the State does not contribute is generally modest (in the order of a few hundred euros, depending on the region).

:::astuce
A careful study is not an ‘extra’ expense: it avoids nasty surprises during the works (missed investigations, unforeseen defects, quotes that spiral). What you invest in the assessment is often recouped on the final cost of the works.
:::

Should you expect the State to fund the study at the same rate as the works? The regulations treat studies and works as eligible expenditure, without always stating a specific rate for the preliminary study. For the exact percentage applicable to *your* study, the only reliable contact is your DRAC: it adjusts the funding to your situation. To understand how these rates work, see also our article [Grant, repayable advance, loan: the differences](/blog/article/subvention-vs-pret).

## Your checklist before you start

:::checklist[What to gather before commissioning the preliminary study]
- The protection order for your property (listed or registered, in whole or in part)
- The contact details of the person dealing with your case at the DRAC
- A description of your project and its scope
- Any archive documents and earlier studies on the property
- The name of a project manager qualified for your property’s status
- The grant application for the study, submitted before the study work starts
:::

## Key takeaways

- The **preliminary study** is the reasoned assessment you commission before restoration works on a historic monument.
- It starts **early**, ahead of the authorisation and the works; it is **exempt from authorisation**.
- On a **listed** property, it falls within the State’s **scientific and technical oversight**, from the earliest stage.
- The project manager depends on the status: an **ACMH** for a publicly owned listed property, a **qualified** architect for a privately owned listed property, an architect without any special qualification for a registered property.
- The study has **its own cost**, eligible for State grants; funding is requested **beforehand**, through an application **separate** from the one for the works.
