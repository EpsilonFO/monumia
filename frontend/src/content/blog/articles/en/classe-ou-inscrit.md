You have just learned that your house, your church or your old mill is ‘classé’ (listed), or perhaps ‘inscrit’ (registered), as a historic monument. These two words keep coming up in official letters, and yet the difference remains unclear. It has very concrete consequences for your works, your formalities and the funding you can claim. Here is what sets them apart, explained simply.

## At a glance

Registration (inscription) and listing (classement) are the **two levels of protection** for historic monuments. Registration is the first level; listing is the highest. The stronger the protection, the more closely the State oversees your works, but the larger the financial support can be. In both cases, routine maintenance remains unrestricted.

:::comparatif{a="Registered property" b="Listed property"}
- Level of protection | First level | The highest
- Authorisation of works | Building permit or prior declaration, with the prefect’s agreement | Specific authorisation from the regional prefect
- Notice before works | Notify the prefect 4 months ahead | Depends on the authorisation issued
- State oversight | Planning consent | Scientific and technical oversight
- Architect | Required, no specialist qualification | Heritage architect, required
:::

## Registration and listing: a question of level

Contrary to a widespread belief, a registered property is not ‘half protected’: it enjoys genuine legal protection. The difference lies in the **degree of heritage interest** recognised in the property.

- **Registration** is the first level of protection. It covers buildings of sufficient artistic or historical interest to make their preservation desirable.
- **Listing** is the highest level. It concerns properties whose conservation is in the public interest from a historical or artistic point of view.

The same property can, moreover, be registered in part (a façade, a roof) and listed in another part. It is the protection decision that specifies what is protected, and at what level.

:::remarque
A property can be **registered in part** (for example its façade) and **listed in another part**. Read your protection order carefully: it is the document that states what is protected, and at what level.
:::

## What listing means in practice

If your property is **listed**, you cannot alter, restore or carry out major repairs on it without an **authorisation issued by the regional prefect** (préfet de région). This authorisation relies on the review carried out by the State departments responsible for historic monuments, within the DRAC (Direction régionale des affaires culturelles, the Ministry of Culture’s regional office).

Three points to remember:

- **Maintenance and routine repairs do not require authorisation.** Maintaining a roof, restoring something like for like: you remain free to do so.
- Major works take place **under the State’s scientific and technical oversight**, from the preliminary studies to the end of the works.
- **Project management** (maîtrise d’œuvre) must be entrusted to an architect with a qualification suited to heritage restoration.

:::attention
**Routine maintenance** remains unrestricted in both cases. But for a registered property, notify the DRAC **at least four months before** any other alteration works: forgetting to do so can hold up your project.
:::

## What changes if your property is registered

For a **registered** property, there is also a framework, but it works through the usual planning permissions:

- Most works other than maintenance require a **building permit** (permis de construire) or a **prior declaration** (déclaration préalable), issued after **agreement from the regional prefect**.
- You must **notify the regional prefect at least four months before** undertaking alteration works.
- An **architect is required** for works that need a building permit, but no specialist qualification is demanded, unlike for listing.

Here again, maintenance and routine repairs remain unrestricted.

## On the funding side: different grant rates

The level of protection directly affects State funding. As a guide, for works on a building, the State’s base grant is around **20% for a registered property** and **35% for a listed property**.

:::barres{unit="%"}
- Registered property: 20
- Listed property: 35
:::

These rates are **adjustable baselines**: they can be raised depending on your situation (opening to the public, an approved management plan, an agreement with the DRAC). For protected movable objects, the base rates are higher (around 30% for registered and 50% for listed).

:::astuce
These percentages are **not automatic**: the grant must be requested **from the DRAC before any works begin**. The right reflex: contact the DRAC **early** in your project, before you even sign a quote.
:::

## The process, step by step (registered property)

:::etapes
1. **Contact the DRAC**: early on, to scope your project and check what is protected.
2. **Notify the regional prefect**: at least four months before the alteration works.
3. **Submit the planning application**: building permit or prior declaration, depending on the scale.
4. **Apply for the grant**: to the DRAC, before the works begin.
:::

## A worked example

Take Madame Martin, the owner of a **registered** barn that she wants to restore. The quote for the eligible works comes to €100,000.

:::chiffres
- **€100,000**: amount of eligible works
- **~€20,000**: estimated State funding (20% base)
- **4 months**: notice to the prefect before the works
:::

As the property is registered, she submits a prior declaration (or a building permit, depending on the scale of the works), notifies the DRAC four months in advance, then applies for a grant. On a 20% base, State funding could amount to around €20,000, possibly more if she opens the property to visitors.

Had the same barn been **listed**, the base grant would rather be around 35%, or about €35,000, but Madame Martin would have had to obtain a specific prefectoral authorisation and entrust the works to a heritage architect. More protection, more funding, but also more requirements.

## Key takeaways

- **Registration** is the first level of protection; **listing** is the highest.
- **Listed** property: authorisation from the regional prefect, the State’s scientific and technical oversight, heritage architect required.
- **Registered** property: building permit or prior declaration with the prefect’s agreement, and notice four months before the works.
- In both cases, **routine maintenance remains unrestricted**.
- Listing opens up **higher grant rates** (around 35% compared with 20% for registered, as a guide).

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a registered property become listed?**
Yes. Registration and listing are two distinct levels, and a property can have its protection strengthened by a later listing if its heritage interest justifies it.

**Do I have to open my property to the public?**
No, protection does not require opening to visitors. However, opening to the public can give access to certain funding or to increased rates.

**Who should I contact about my works?**
Your main contact is the **DRAC** (Direction régionale des affaires culturelles) in your region, and more specifically its conservation régionale des monuments historiques (the regional historic monuments department). Get in touch with them before any project.
