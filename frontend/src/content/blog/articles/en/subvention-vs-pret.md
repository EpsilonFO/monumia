Two letters arrive on the same morning. The first awards you State funding to restore your roof: money you will never pay back. The second offers you finance at an attractive rate: money that you will, on the other hand, have to repay over fifteen or twenty years. Both talk about ‘support for your project’. Yet one lightens your budget for good, while the other merely spreads the burden. Knowing how to tell what you keep from what you must pay back changes everything in a funding plan.

## Two different logics

The word ‘funding’ (aide) covers opposite realities. To find your way, remember three families.

A **grant** (subvention) is financial support that you do not have to repay. When the State, through your DRAC (Direction régionale des affaires culturelles, the Ministry of Culture’s regional office), subsidises works on a historic monument, the sum paid is non-repayable (‘à fonds perdu’): it permanently reduces your expenditure. According to the Ministry of Culture, however, the award of a grant remains ‘a possibility and not a right’, granted within the limits of available funds. You do not receive it automatically: you apply for it, and the authorities decide.

A **repayable advance** (avance remboursable) looks like a grant on the day it is paid, but it eventually goes back. It is a sum advanced to get your project started, which you then repay over time, often interest-free and without any guarantee required. It relieves you in the short term; it does not reduce the final cost of your project.

A **loan** (prêt) is standard bank finance: you are lent a capital sum that you repay, plus interest, according to a repayment schedule. There are loans dedicated to heritage, on gentler terms than the market, but their nature remains the same: it is borrowed money, and therefore money to be paid back.

:::remarque
The dividing line is neither the amount nor the body paying it: it is the question of repayment. A grant reduces your expenditure for ever. An advance or a loan only shifts the moment when you pay the money out.
:::

## The comparison

Here are the two ends of the spectrum, side by side: the State grant, which you keep, and the heritage loan, which you repay. The repayable advance sits between the two, paid out like the first and paid back like the second.

:::comparatif{a="Grant (DRAC)" b="Heritage loan" display="cartes"}
- Repayable? | No, permanent funding | Yes, capital and interest
- Effect on the final cost | Reduces the expenditure for good | Does not reduce the cost, spreads it over time
- Who pays | The State through the DRAC | A bank or the Banque des Territoires
- Eligibility | Protected property, approved project, funds available | Ability to repay, eligible project
- Time to obtain | Several months, application before works | Faster, bank logic
- Guarantee required | None | Depends on the lender
:::

For a property protected as a historic monument, the State grant represents on average, according to the Ministry of Culture, around **40% for a listed (classé) property** and **20% for a registered (inscrit) property** (with a cap that can reach 40% of actual expenditure). These rates are indicative baselines, adjusted to your situation: contact your DRAC for the figure that will apply to your project.

On the loan side, the Banque des Territoires (the public lending arm of the Caisse des Dépôts) has launched a ‘Prêt Patrimoine’ (heritage loan) for the rehabilitation of properties listed as historic monuments or located in a site patrimonial remarquable (outstanding heritage site) and open to the public. It can run for up to 25 years, at a rate indexed to the Livret A (a regulated French savings account). A loan of this kind does not replace a grant: it funds the share that remains for you to pay once the grants have been deducted.

:::exergue[€0]
That is what a grant ultimately costs you: nothing to repay, ever. A loan, however attractive, always leaves you with capital and interest to pay. That is the factor that should guide your decision above everything else.
:::

## When should you choose one or the other?

The right answer depends on your property, your timetable and your cash flow.

**Look for a grant first.** It is always the most worthwhile form of funding, since it is not repaid. If your property is listed or registered, a DRAC grant should be your first reflex. One rule is decisive here: the application must be submitted **before any works begin**, usually in the year preceding the operation. Starting the works before submitting your application will cost you the funding.

:::attention[The timing trap]
A grant cannot be claimed after the fact. If you start the works before submitting your application to the DRAC, you will no longer be able to claim it for that operation. Contact your DRAC early, before you even sign a quote.
:::

**Consider a loan for the remaining balance.** Even with generous grants, a restoration project leaves a share to be financed. That is where a loan (a standard bank loan or a dedicated heritage loan) makes sense: it lets you carry out the works without using up all your savings at once. You pay for the convenience of spreading the cost, but the total cost increases by the interest.

**A repayable advance helps when cash is temporarily short.** Some local authorities or foundations offer advances to get works started, until the grants are actually paid. Useful for bridging a cash-flow gap, as long as you do not forget that the money will go back.

If your property is **not protected** as a historic monument, the DRAC will not step in, but other doors are open. The Fondation du patrimoine (a French heritage foundation), for example, awards funding and a label that open the way to sponsorship and tax benefits, particularly for unprotected built heritage. Find out what applies to the exact nature of your property.

## Can you combine them?

Yes, and it is the rule rather than the exception. Restoring a monument almost always relies on a **multi-layered funding plan**: a State grant, often supplemented by grants from the region, the département or the municipality, then a loan or personal contribution to cover the remaining balance. The Ministry of Culture even provides that, when a local authority is carrying out the works, public funders combine around a minimum threshold of local contribution.

In practice, grants and loans are not mutually exclusive: they occupy different layers of the same plan. The grant reduces the expenditure; the loan finances what remains. That is precisely the role of co-financing, which we explain in detail in our article [Co-financing explained simply](/blog/article/cofinancement).

:::astuce
Before signing anything, set out your funding plan: total cost of the works, expected grants, remaining balance, then a loan or advance to fill the gap. You will then know exactly what you keep and what you will have to pay back.
:::

## Key takeaways

- A **grant** is not repaid: it reduces your expenditure for good. It is the funding to aim for first.
- A **repayable advance** relieves you for the duration of the works, but goes back afterwards, generally interest-free.
- A **loan** spreads the burden without lightening it: you repay the capital plus interest.
- For a protected property, the DRAC grant is around **40% (listed)** or **20% (registered)** as a guide, and must be requested **before the works**.
- Grants and loans **can be combined**: the first reduces the expenditure, the second finances the remaining balance.
