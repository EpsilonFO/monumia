The DRAC: behind the acronym, who actually decides on your works? You have probably come across these four letters in a letter, a grant application form or a reply from your town hall: ‘get in touch with the DRAC’. Many owners then find themselves a little lost, somewhere between the municipality, the prefect, the Architecte des Bâtiments de France (the state heritage architect) and this department whose role nobody has ever really explained to them. Here, without jargon, is what the DRAC is for and when it comes into play in your project.

## What is the DRAC?

The **DRAC** (Direction régionale des affaires culturelles, the regional directorate of cultural affairs) is the State department that carries out the Ministry of Culture’s policy at the level of your region. There is one per region. Placed under the authority of the regional prefect (préfet de région), it covers a broad field: live performance, books, archives, archaeology… and, what concerns you here, **heritage** and **historic monuments**.

Its mission is set out in an official text, the decree of 8 June 2010 on the organisation of the DRACs.

:::citation[Decree no. 2010-633 of 8 June 2010, article 2 (unofficial translation)]
The regional directorate of cultural affairs is responsible for conducting the State’s cultural policy in the region and the départements that make it up, in particular in the fields of knowledge, protection, conservation and promotion of heritage.
:::

Remember these four words (knowledge, protection, conservation, promotion): they sum up exactly what the DRAC does for a protected property. For an owner, it is therefore the **main point of contact** as soon as a listed (classé) or registered (inscrit) monument is involved.

Within the DRAC, two departments are particularly relevant to you. The **conservation régionale des monuments historiques** (CRMH, the regional historic monuments department) looks after the protected monuments themselves: it is the CRMH that examines your applications for works and grants on a listed or registered property. The **unités départementales de l’architecture et du patrimoine** (UDAP, the architecture and heritage offices), one per département, house the **Architectes des Bâtiments de France** (ABF) and, for their part, watch over the *surroundings* of monuments and protected areas.

:::remarque
The DRAC is regional, the UDAP works at département level. In practice, for a protected property, you write to the CRMH at your DRAC. For a project located *near* a monument without being protected itself, you are more likely to deal with the Architecte des Bâtiments de France, through the UDAP of your département. Both come under the same ministry, but they do not handle the same cases.
:::

## In practice, what does it do for your property?

If your property is protected as a historic monument, the DRAC supports you throughout your project, not just on the day the works begin. Its action takes several forms.

**It advises you early on.** Before you even sign a quote, the CRMH can tell you exactly what is protected (the whole property, or only a façade or a roof), what is already known about your monument and which studies are expected given the nature of the works you have in mind. It is the right first step.

**It authorises and supervises the works.** On a **listed** property, the highest level of protection, you cannot alter it or carry out major restoration without an authorisation, examined by the DRAC’s departments and issued by the regional prefect. The works then take place under the State’s **scientific and technical oversight**, from the preliminary studies through to handover. On a **registered** property, the first level of protection, the framework is lighter but real: you must notify the authorities before any alteration works.

**It examines State funding applications.** The DRAC receives and reviews your grant applications for studies and works on a protected property. The ministry points out that a grant remains ‘a possibility and not a right’, awarded within the limits of available funds, and the DRAC adjusts the rate to your situation. One rule never changes: the application must be submitted **before** you start what it pays for.

:::attention[The timing trap]
A DRAC grant cannot be claimed after the fact. If you start the works before submitting your application, you risk losing the funding for that operation. Contact the DRAC early, before you even sign a quote.
:::

## Should you contact the DRAC or the town hall?

This is the most common confusion. The rule comes down to one simple question: is your property itself protected, or merely *close to* a protected monument?

:::decision[DRAC, town hall or ABF: who should you contact?]
- Is your property listed or registered as a historic monument? | oui: the DRAC (regional historic monuments department) examines your works and your funding applications | non: continue below
- Is your property within the surroundings of a monument (500 m radius, with intervisibility) or in a protected area? | oui: the Architecte des Bâtiments de France (UDAP) gives an opinion on your planning application | non: your town hall is enough, under ordinary planning rules
:::

A few pointers to find your way. If your property is **protected**, the DRAC is your point of entry, even if you also submit a planning application to the town hall. If your property is not protected but lies within the **surroundings** of a historic monument (generally a 500-metre radius, where there is intervisibility with the monument), it is the **Architecte des Bâtiments de France** who gives an opinion on your application for a building permit (permis de construire) or prior declaration (déclaration préalable). Finally, if your property is neither protected nor within a protection perimeter, your **town hall** handles your application under the usual planning rules.

:::remarque
In every case, the town hall remains the place where you submit your planning applications (building permit, prior declaration). The DRAC and the ABF do not process planning applications in place of the municipality: they step in on top of it, for heritage protection purposes. The mayor remains your local point of contact, including to find out whether your plot falls within a protection perimeter.
:::

To tell these levels of protection and their consequences apart, you can read our articles [Listed or registered: what’s the difference?](/blog/article/classe-ou-inscrit) and [The surroundings of a historic monument](/blog/article/perimetre-et-abords).

## How to contact it

There is one DRAC per region, each with its own site on the Ministry of Culture portal (culture.gouv.fr). For a protected property, ask for the **conservation régionale des monuments historiques**: that is the department that will follow your case.

Before getting in touch, gather what you need to identify your property. You will save time, and so will the person you deal with.

:::checklist[What to gather before contacting the DRAC]
- The protection order for your property (listed or registered, in whole or in part)
- The exact address and cadastral reference of your property
- A description of your project and its scope
- Photographs of its current condition
- Any studies or archives already available on the monument
:::

:::astuce
If you do not yet know whether your property is protected, start with your town hall: it can tell you whether your plot is covered by a historic monument or a protection perimeter. You will then know who to turn to: the DRAC, the ABF or the ordinary planning department.
:::

## Key takeaways

- The **DRAC** is the State’s regional department for culture; for heritage, its role covers the knowledge, protection, conservation and promotion of protected properties.
- For a **listed or registered** property, your contact is the DRAC’s **regional historic monuments department** (CRMH): it advises, authorises works and examines grant applications.
- For a property **near** a monument (surroundings, intervisibility), it is the **Architecte des Bâtiments de France**, through the UDAP, who gives an opinion.
- The **town hall** remains the place to submit planning applications and a good starting point to find out whether your property is affected.
- Contact the DRAC **early**, especially for a grant: funding must be requested before the works.
